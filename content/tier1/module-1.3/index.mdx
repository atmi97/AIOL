# Module 1.3 — AI Risks and Limitations

### 1.3.1 Hallucinations

A hallucination is AI-generated content that is presented confidently, sounds plausible, and is factually wrong. It is the single most important failure mode to understand, because it is the one most likely to land a stakeholder-facing error in a document that looks professional.

#### Why they happen

Generative AI models are trained to produce fluent, plausible text. When the model is uncertain — when the actual answer is not strongly represented in its training data, or the question is ambiguous, or the requested output requires precise recall — the model does not stop. It fills the gap with content that fits the pattern of a correct answer without being a correct answer. From the outside, you cannot tell the difference.

The key point: AI does not have a separate mechanism for "I don't know." Its default behaviour is to produce fluent output. Uncertainty is something you have to check for — the model will not flag it for you.

#### What hallucinations look like in an AMS context

- A Copilot-generated financial summary that references a quarter that does not exist, or cites a figure that is plausible in magnitude but fabricated.
- A drafted vendor correspondence that refers to a contract clause that is not in the actual contract.
- A ServiceNow knowledge response that confidently describes a reset procedure that was deprecated two years ago.
- A procurement recommendation that cites a supplier scoring framework the organization has never used.
- A policy citation — "per Section 7.4 of the 3sHealth Payroll Handbook" — when Section 7.4 does not exist.
- An Oracle Fusion report narrative that invents an employee ID or references an organizational unit that was decommissioned.
**Guardrail — three questions to ask about any factual claim in an AI output<br />1. Where did this come from? Does the AI cite a source that I can actually check?<br />2. Does it match what I can verify in the authoritative system — Oracle, ServiceNow, the policy library?<br />3. If I disagree with one small detail, does the whole output still hold up, or was the AI just pattern-matching?<br />If you cannot answer these, do not use the output.**

### 1.3.2 Bias

Bias is a different failure mode. Where hallucination is about made-up specifics, bias is about systematic skew in outputs that reflects skew in the training data. Bias is often subtle — the output looks correct, but it consistently favours or disfavours certain groups, options, or framings.

#### How bias gets into AI

AI learns from historical data. If the data reflects historical inequity, the model reflects that inequity too. A résumé screening tool trained on ten years of past hiring decisions will reproduce patterns in those decisions — including ones the organization is actively trying to move away from. A procurement recommender trained on past vendor selections will tend to recommend vendors that look like past vendors, which may or may not align with current supplier diversity goals.

#### Where bias shows up in admin AI

| Area | Type of bias to watch for |
| --- | --- |
| Recruitment and screening | Gender, age, or name-based patterns in how candidates are ranked or summarized. Language models trained on English-language web text also carry implicit cultural biases. |
| Performance review drafting | AI-drafted review text can use systematically different language for different demographics — stronger achievement language for some groups, more hedging for others. |
| Pay equity analysis | An AI summary of pay data can obscure structural disparities if it is prompted to produce an "executive overview" rather than a break-out by demographic group. |
| Procurement / vendor selection | Vendor scoring models trained on historical contracts can reinforce incumbency and disadvantage newer or smaller suppliers — including Indigenous-owned businesses that 3sHealth has commitments to engage with. |
| Service desk routing | If past routing data reflects systematic mishandling — e.g., tickets from French-speaking users getting de-prioritized — an AI router trained on that data can automate the bias. |
| Documentation language | Generative AI often defaults to an American English, corporate-tone register. 3sHealth content frequently needs Canadian English and an accessibility-forward tone; AI output often needs editing to match. |


### 1.3.3 Errors in admin context

Beyond hallucination and bias, there are other ordinary failure modes. They matter in administrative platforms because the outputs are almost always someone's money, employment, or vendor relationship.

#### Calculation errors

Generative AI is notoriously inconsistent at arithmetic. It can correctly restate a policy but incorrectly add a column of numbers. Copilot in Excel has been improving rapidly and delegates math to spreadsheet functions where it can — but "the AI did the math" is never a sufficient audit explanation on a payroll or invoice calculation. If the numbers matter, they must be verified.

#### Formatting errors that read as content errors

AI outputs frequently invent structure — headings, bullet points, summary tables — that look authoritative but do not match the original source. A summary of a 40-page policy that produces a tidy three-bullet recap can hide material nuance. The summary may be accurate in what it includes and misleading in what it leaves out.

#### Staleness

Large language models have a training cutoff — a date past which they know nothing. If you ask a model about a 2026 HIPA Regulation amendment and the model's knowledge stops in 2024, it will either say it does not know (good) or produce something plausible anyway (not good). Even connected AI — Copilot that can search your tenant — has the same issue when it synthesizes a response.

#### Context window limits

AI tools have a finite amount of input they can consider at once. If you feed a 200-page document into a tool designed for 40 pages, the model may quietly drop content from the middle. The output will still look complete.

#### Prompt sensitivity

The same question asked slightly differently can produce meaningfully different answers. "Summarize this invoice" and "List the key points of this invoice" may emphasize different fields. This is not a bug you will fix — it is a property of how these systems work — but it is a reason to be cautious about treating any single AI output as definitive.

### 1.3.4 The confidence problem

Every failure mode in this module shares a common feature: the AI does not flag its own uncertainty. It uses the same fluent, confident tone for a correct statement and a fabricated one. This is not dishonesty — the model has no model of truth — it is just how these systems are trained to produce output.

The practical implication: the standard workplace habit of trusting a well-written document does not work for AI output. A confident tone is evidence of nothing. Only verification is evidence.

**The single habit this module is asking you to build<br />Treat every AI output as a draft from a colleague who is enthusiastic, well-spoken, and occasionally makes things up with great confidence. Your name is on the final version. Verify before you send.<br />Tier 2 introduces a formal framework for this verification — FACTS (Factual accuracy, Attribution, Completeness, Tone, Safety). For Tier 1, the habit is enough.**

### 1.3.5 Activity: "Spot the hallucination"

The instructional designer building this activity will produce three realistic AI-generated outputs, each containing 2–4 deliberately planted errors. A draft specification for the three scenarios follows. Facilitator notes and answer keys are maintained in the companion activity pack.

Scenario A — Financial summary narrative

An AI-generated executive summary of the Q2 2026 financial performance for a 3sHealth service line. Planted errors: (1) a quarter-over-quarter variance stated as percent where the figure is clearly basis points; (2) a reference to a budget line that does not exist in the Fusion chart of accounts; (3) a citation to "Board Resolution 2025-14" where no such resolution exists; (4) a plausible but fabricated comparison to a prior-year benchmark.

Scenario B — HR policy response

An AI-generated response to an employee enquiry about bereavement leave. Planted errors: (1) a day-count that is not in 3sHealth's actual policy; (2) an invented reference to Section 8.2 of the Employee Handbook; (3) a statement that leave is approved "automatically" when policy requires manager approval.

Scenario C — Procurement recommendation

An AI-generated vendor recommendation for a linen-services regional contract. Planted errors: (1) an invented vendor scoring framework; (2) a reference to a procurement round that closed on a date before it actually opened; (3) a recommendation that does not account for 3sHealth's Indigenous supplier engagement commitments.

### 1.3.6 Module summary

- Hallucinations — confident, fluent, factually wrong — are the most important AI failure mode to internalize.
- Bias is a different failure mode: systematic skew from skewed training data. Particularly watch for it in recruitment, performance language, pay equity, procurement, and service desk routing.
- AI also fails at arithmetic, invents structure, goes stale, drops middle content in long documents, and is sensitive to phrasing.
- AI does not signal its own uncertainty. Confident tone is evidence of nothing.
- The single Tier 1 habit to build: verify every AI output before you act on it or send it.
